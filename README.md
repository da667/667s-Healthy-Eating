## General Advice

This section details some general advice that has served me well on my journey towards better new nutrition. Hope you find it helpful!

### Keep a Food Journal

Best advice I can offer you is to keep track of everything ~~put in your mouth~~ eat. How you do that is entirely up to you, but here are some helpful guidelines:

- **There are fitness apps for your smart phones.** And they might even be pretty good, and have a huge database full of data about the calories and/or nutritional macros that might even make using them look attractive, and save you time. **Avoid Them**. Why? One word: **Enshitification**. Every single weight loss and tracking app I've seen that loads on a smart phone either starts off totally free and amazing, or starts off as freemium slop. And they both get progressively worse, as they get more and more attention. Attention attracts money. Money attracts shareholders. Shareholders want to extract more money out of a good thing, and end up trying to squeeze blood out of a stone, turning the entire application into a micro-transaction shitfest. See also: Every streaming service, and every single AAA gaming publisher. **Fuck em.** You don't need them. Use a general note-taking app instead. Preferably one that is multi-platform and/or indexes your notes so you can search through your past notes quickly.

- **There's a good chance if you work in tech in a sedantary position, you have a note-taking, word processing, IDE, or organizer app of some sort you use already.** Could be notepad. Could be word. Could be cloud solutions like Office365, Google Apps, Cryptpad, etc. For all I care, it could be VIM, EMACs, or your preferred IDE. Personally I use [Obsidian](https://obsidian.md/download). Its free, its multi-platform, it supports markdown, has plugin support, and if you want to pay for it, can be made to synchronize with your other systems. Or, if you are the technomancer I know you are if you are reading this, *you'll find ways* to sync your files between devices for free -- VPN connection home to a fileshare with your Obsidian files, [syncthing](https://syncthing.net/), public cloud services, [self-hosted](https://owncloud.com/) [cloud services](https://cryptpad.fr), whatever. You and I both know that if there is a will, we find a way, because that's usually our job: Making shit work with hopes, prayers, and gratuitous amounts of elbow grease.

- **Turning your food journal into a near-daily routine is one of the hardest parts to consistently achieve.** Especially since at first, you're going to have to pore over the nutrition labels of everything you want to eat, and consistently commit them to your note-taking app. You might also have to bust out the calculator to calculate all of the macros, of all of the combined ingredients for meals you will be cooking, and portions you'll be consuming. But once you actually achieve making it a routine, and you get enough data entered for the items you eat on a regular basis, it gets easier. Not only that, it feels weird when you don't do it. For instances on my cheat day -- more on that in a moment.

- Related to keeping your food journal, plan your meals in advance, as much as you can. For me, that usually means that we'll make plans for dinner throughout the week, and I'll keep my breakfast/lunch open, depending on how many calories, sodium, sugar, phosphates, or potassium is going to be in my dinner, because when you got as many problems as I do, you have to be cognizant of all that (and more).

- So, if you know that tuesday is taco tuesday, and you know you're gonna be using that taco seasoning mix packet that's 300 mg of sodium per serving, and that the packet has six servings, and you're diving your dinner between two people, you'll know that you'll be using a minimum of 900mg of sodium for your dinner, not counting the rest of your meal items already. Your dinner is going to contain most of the [2300mg maximum recommended value](https://www.heart.org/en/healthy-living/healthy-eating/eat-smart/sodium/how-much-sodium-should-i-eat-per-day) for an adult. Don't want to commit all of your daily allotted sodium to a single meal? You're going to have to learn to make "smart trades", and and use alternatives to fulfill your needs. We'll talk about that later, because that's the basis for the recipes I'm throwing together, they all came from a lot of data entry, a lot of searching through grocery store websites, and a lot of looking at nutrition labels.

### Incorporate physical activity into your life

Well, no shit, sherlock. Technically, everyone will tell you that, if you create a calorie deficit by eating less than what your body needs to maintain its current weight, your body will burn the fat in order to make up the difference, but physical activity is really good for your heart, and of course, maintaining your flexibility. The American Heart Association recommends at least [2.5 hours of cardio per week](https://www.heart.org/en/healthy-living/fitness/fitness-basics/aha-recs-for-physical-activity-in-adults). You and I are probably round-the-clock professionals. Most of us in this industry have a one-track mind, and will not or cannot stop until the end of their shift, or until the problem is fixed. I completely understand that. But, find a way to find your physical activity into any time slot you have available.

Now, 2.5 hours a week sounds daunting, but that's 25 minutes, six times a week. Now, if you're morbidly obese like me, even that might be a significant ask. I understand and there is zero judgement to be had in here. **Start small. Do what you can. Work your way up as you go.** When I started doing cardio again, I was doing a 20-minute session every six days, and after 15 minutes, my back and calf muscles would lock up. I would pause my video routine, sit down for a minute to relieve my cramped/locked muscles, then continue when I could control those muscles, and finish my workout. This leads me to another piece of advice: **You don't have to do all of your cardio/workout routine in one sitting.** There are many who will tell you that you have to work out x number of minutes before you begin reaping the benefits of working out. **Fuck Them. Figure out the pace and timing that fits your body's needs.** I started only being able to workout for 15 minutes out of my 20 before stopping. Then I reached being able to work out the full 20 minutes. Then I added on another 15 minutes. Then I added on another 10 minutes, three days a week. 35 minutes 3 days a week, and 45 minutes another 3 days of the week is 4 hours of physical activity, and it doesn't need to be a marathon herculean effort that involves going to the gym and having to get it all done at once.

Personally, I use Leslie Sansone's Cardio walking routines available on youtube. Its cardio, its low impact, she has a variety of videos with various different lengths of time, it involves moving most parts of the body in a variety of directions, getting every muscle involved, and what's more is that you can mute the videos, follow the visual cues, and just put on whatever music you like. Before you know it, your workout(s) are over.

If you prefer to use an exercise bike, an elliptical, a treadmill, swimming/treading water/walking in a pool or other methods of cardio activity, that's fine. **Do the activity that works for you. Any amount and level activity is better than zero activity.** Just like with the food journal, establishing your routine is going to be the absolute hardest part of your fitness journey. But again, once you get past a certain point, even if you're tired, you'll make time before the end of the day to get your routine done, or you'll feel weird. Or even worse, you'll be dragging ass the next day to struggle through your routine. **This is normal. It is okay If you aren't pedaling quite as fast, swinging your arms quite as wide, walking quite as fast, or swimming quite as well as you normally do, that is fine. Any activity you can perform to maintain your routine is good activity.** However, if you are physically unable to even shuffle your body even slightly, to maintain your routine, that is also okay. Learn to forgive yourself, and get back to it when you can do so **SAFELY, ACCORDING TO YOUR MEDICAL ADVISORS**.

### Drink Water, damn you

Water is a vital fluid our body absolutely needs to for a variety of critical functions. By weight, we are all _primarily_ water ballons. Standard advice seems to be `8x8`, which is 8 servings of 8 fluid ounces (8 x 8 is 64 fluid ounces -- or for those of you who carry those gigantic water stanley cups or large water containers like I do, two of those giant 32oz containers.), However, the reality is, most people need [70 to 100 fluid ounces of water per day for optimal operation.](https://www.eatright.org/health/essential-nutrients/water/how-much-water-do-you-need). The good news is, by water, they mean consuming just about anything with fluid in it. Coffee, tea, juice, juicy fruits (apples, grapes, berries, watermelon, tomato, etc.), juicy veggies (cucumber, lettuce, etc.) , etc. Even though soda has water in it, it should come as no surprise that you really need to limit your soda intake and/or sugary juices. Yes, even if they're diet. Notice how I said _limit_ your intake, not _completely remove_ them from your life. Check out the 80/20 section below, and if your medical advisors say its safe, indulge once in a while.

### Get used to looking at nutrition labels for food items

When recording the food you eat, get use to looking at nutrition labels. Okay, so what if you're at your desk, you have to enter some food you just ate, and you really don't want to get up and go to the cupboard, the fridge, or god forbid, fish a wrapper out of the trash can for whatever thing(s) you just ate? The miracle of the modern internet is that many grocery stores offer online ordering now. And by extension, many of those stores now allow you to view nutrition labels for foods as you purchase them. For example, [walmart](https://walmart.com) allows one to search for a variety of food items without logging in to an app, or anything, and will have a number of pictures of the food items they sell (that aren't sold by a third party or something). You can use this to tabulate macros for recipes you bake, and figure out if there are viable alternatives, such as low sugar/no sugar added, no salt added, etc. Additionally, many fast food or restaurant chains will have a nutrition information site, or offer a PDF that contains nutrition information for their offerings. Prepare carefully. Many big menu items have fucktons of sodium in the seasonings and flavorings they use for their food. Subs and sandwich shops are especially notorious for claiming that they're all about healthy and wholesome living, while serving you sandwiches and subs that are rich in phosphates, and 3000+mg of sodium in a meal. Welcome to America - Land of the free, Home of the Heart Disease. It never hurts to ask if you can request no salt/sugar/spices added, and just use a tiny sprinkle of table salt or sugar instead.

### Do not trust "Reduced Sugar" or "Low/Reduced Sodium" items

Let me give you an example of this one. A typical cup of Walmart's generic chicken stock is **990mg of sodium PER.CUP.**. Reduced sodium chicken stock is **500-ish mg of sodium per cup, depending on brand**. No salt added varieties? about 90-110mg of sodium per cup. The same caveat applies to sugary items as well. Look at the nutrition labels. **Don't assume that if its labeled "Heart Healthy" or "Reduced" anything, that its remotely healthy for you. READ THE GODDAMN NUTRITION LABELS.** If you have the calories/sugar/sodium available to absorb the cost of said food? Fine, use it as you see fit. But be sure to pay attention.

### When using Fruits or Veggies, try to get Fresh. If not Fresh then Frozen. If not Frozen, then "No salt added"

Just as the title says, There are many many canned fruit or vegetable products you'll consider buying that, at face value sound really good, and really healthy. And then you look at the label, and find out that the 29oz can of tomato sauce you use as a base for your sphaghetti has 290mg of sodium per serving, contains 13 servings, and eating all of it will net you 3,380mg of sodium, before you even think about adding anything else to your sauce. Whereas a can of no salt added tomato sauce is 15mg of sodium per serving, with a total of 195mg of sodium per can. **Learn to start with no salt/sugar added items, then add in the salt or sugar yourself, as necessary** not only is much healthier, you get to control how intense the salt flavor is. If you have to purchase items that are canned, and are loaded with sodium, and you can do so, rinse or soak the veggies/fruit in water. This can remove a portion of the sodium/sugar and is better than doing nothing. If you have to purchase canned fruit, purchase fruit preserved in its own juices, NOT in syrup.

### If you're trying to replace salt and sugar in your recipes, spice up your life

Variety is the spice of life, and adding seasoning to your food is the variety you may need to help limit your salt and/or sugar intake. Notice how I said _limit_ again, and not _completely remove_. As humans, we all tend to love salt and sugar, and our body requires it in some way, shape, or form. Finding spice blends that incorporate salt is a perfectly good strategy: You still get a little bit of salt to enhance the flavor of your food, but you are consuming less than say, cracking a boatload of salt into your food, or like I use to, dumping loads of seasoning salt into it, instead. 

### Downward trends, not constant progress

Due to how our bodies work, do not expect perfect, constant progress, especially if you're looking to reduce your weight, A1C, blood pressure, or excessive amounts of electrolytes. Our bodies work in mysterious ways, and will retain weight, nutrients, or blood pressure at extremely inopportune times. Like, weigh-in day, or time of day you performing your BP check. We are looking for downward trends, and eventual progress, not a perfect downward slide.  There are weeks where I have lost 5lbs, followed by weeks where I gained it all back. Followed by the week after that in which I lost all of it again, plus another two lbs, for 7lbs of total loss. Excessive sodiun intake leads to excessive water retention. Undereating can lead to excessive fat and nutrient retention. Your different sources of stress at different period of your life can affect your Blood Pressure measures. Weigh in either well before, or well after cheat days. Take your blood pressure measurements in a place as quiet and as peaceful as you can. I know this sounds stupid, but for me thinking of happy thoughts, good memories or my favorite places to be, and taking three deep breathes to calm myself helps me before taking my daily Blood Pressure checks. Don't get upset if progress isn't what you want it to be. Work with your healthcare advisors, and look for downward trends. **Be forgiving of yourself.##

### 80/20

80/20 is, and I'm paraphrasing here, a concept that tells us that we do not have to be firing on all cylinders 100% of the time. Occasionally allow yourself leniency and forgiveness. Allow yourself some flex-space and time for acheiving your goals. For me, this manifests as a weekly "cheat day". I don't work out on Sundays. Additionally if there is a particular meal that I'm craving that I know is not something that should be a part of my regular diet, Sunday is the day I'm eating it. That's also the day that I'm not writing it down or tracking it. Then, that coming Monday, I'm back to my routine again. Maybe with a little extra water to help flush out excess sugar or sodium from my cheat day. All of us are human. 100% compliance towards a new dietary and exercise lifestyle change is commendable, but wanting to be rewarded for progress, or wanting to eat your favorite meals isn't a crime. Of course as with every other piece of advice here, consult with your medical advisors before you decide to smash down a bowl of ramen that's 3500mg in one sitting. If your doctor says "It's okay, very very occasionally", savor it. You earned it.
